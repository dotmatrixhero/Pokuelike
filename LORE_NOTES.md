# Lore notes: canon worth mining, and what's only theory

Reference notes for the campaign and humans design, gathered because this
project's premise — a pre-trainer world where people fear Pokémon and
nobody has ever bonded with one — sits right on top of some real canon that
is worth knowing rather than accidentally reinventing.

**Three confidence tiers, labelled throughout**, because mixing them is how
you end up building a world on a creepypasta:

- **[CANON]** — actual in-game text, dex entries or plot.
- **[THEORY]** — fan theory, some better supported than others.
- **[FICTION]** — fan-made, culturally famous, not real.

A note on my own limits: I'm confident on Gen 1–8 and Legends: Arceus, and
reasonably confident on Gen 9 / Paldea. For the most recent titles I'd
rather flag uncertainty than invent detail — anything below that isn't
labelled is something I'd want double-checked before it becomes load-bearing.

---

## The deep past, by in-world chronology

Direct clarification on what's wanted here: *"chronologically older, but
developed from more recent games."* That's the interesting axis, because the
franchise's deep past was almost entirely written **backwards** — Gen 1 had
essentially no ancient history, and every generation since has excavated a
layer underneath the last one.

Rough in-world order, oldest first, tagged with the game that actually
revealed it:

| In-world era | What | Revealed by |
|---|---|---|
| Before everything | Arceus hatches in nothingness; creates Dialga, Palkia, Giratina; the lake trio give humanity knowledge, emotion, will | Gen 4 (2006), deepened by Legends: Arceus |
| Deep prehistory | **Paradox Pokémon** — ancient (and future) forms utterly unlike modern ones, sealed in Area Zero | Gen 9 (2022) |
| Ancient | **The Celestica** — a people who could commune with Pokémon, whose ruins litter what becomes Sinnoh | Legends: Arceus (2022) |
| ~3000 years ago | **The Kalos war** and AZ's ultimate weapon, fuelled by Pokémon lives | Gen 6 (2013) |
| ~3000 years ago | **Galar's Darkest Day** — Eternatus arrives, two heroes stop it | Gen 8 (2019) |
| Ancient | **Unova's original dragon** splits into Reshiram and Zekrom; Kyurem is the husk | Gen 5 (2010) |
| Ancient | **The Regis sealed away** by people who could bind legendaries; Braille as a lost script | Gen 3 (2002) |
| Ancient | **Alola's island conflict**; the tapu; Necrozma, which lost its light | Gen 7 (2016) |
| Pre-modern | **Hisui** — frontier Sinnoh, Poké Balls newly invented, people afraid of Pokémon | Legends: Arceus (2022) |
| ~150 years pre-Gen 2 | The **Brass Tower fire**; Ho-Oh resurrects the beast trio | Gen 2 (1999) |
| Modern | Gens 1–9 | — |

### The pattern worth stealing outright

Look at the table and the franchise's actual method becomes obvious:

> **Every modern power system is the residue of an ancient catastrophe.**

- **Mega Evolution stones** are debris from AZ's weapon blast.
- **Dynamax** is tied to the Darkest Day and Eternatus.
- **Terastallization** comes out of the Area Zero crater.
- **Ho-Oh's resurrection** of the beast trio is a fire that killed three
  Pokémon.

That is *exactly* the shape of the TMs-as-ancient-relics proposal in
HUMANS_DESIGN.md. Not a departure from canon — the house style. If our
world's move-teaching relics are the leftovers of something that went badly
wrong, we are doing precisely what every generation since Gen 3 has done.

A second, subtler pattern: **the deep past is always revealed
archaeologically**, through ruins, murals, Braille, folk tales in a library
and diaries in a burnt-out lab — almost never through a character
explaining it. Our generated-history-plus-ruins-plus-chronicle stack is a
machine for exactly this, and the discipline to copy is the *restraint*.

### Hisui / Legends: Arceus [CANON] — read this section first

**This is essentially our premise, already shipped by Game Freak.** Not a
problem, but it's the difference between deliberately building on a proven
setup and accidentally rebuilding one.

- **Hisui is ancient Sinnoh** — a frontier, roughly Meiji-era Japanese in
  flavour (a Hokkaido/Ezo analogue), long before the modern region.
- **Poké Balls were just invented.** Hand-crafted from apricorns and
  tumblestones, made of wood and metal, produced one at a time. New
  technology, not infrastructure.
- **People are genuinely afraid of Pokémon.** This is the whole premise.
  Ordinary villagers don't have partners; the idea that you could work
  alongside one is radical and unproven.
- **Jubilife Village** is a frontier settlement behind a wooden palisade,
  run by the **Galaxy Expedition Team** — settlers from elsewhere, organised
  into Survey, Security, Medical and Supply corps.
- **Two clans live closer to the land**: the **Diamond Clan** (reveres time
  / Dialga) and the **Pearl Clan** (reveres space / Palkia), both invoking
  "Almighty Sinnoh."
- **Noble Pokémon and their wardens.** Certain Pokémon are touched by a
  mysterious power and revered; a human **warden** tends each one. When a
  noble goes **frenzied**, you don't kill it — you calm it, by throwing
  balms made of its favourite food. A revered animal that has gone wrong and
  must be soothed rather than slain.
- **Space-time distortions** — rifts where out-of-place Pokémon appear.
  **Alphas** are oversized, red-eyed, aggressive individuals.
- **The Celestica** — an *even older* civilisation whose ruins are scattered
  across Hisui, connected to Arceus, who could commune with Pokémon. Hisui
  itself is already living among the wreckage of something older.
- The player arrives through a rift, sent by Arceus. **Volo** is the twist
  antagonist, wanting the world remade.

**Three attitudes toward Pokémon, coexisting in one setting**: the villagers
fear them, the clans revere them, the expedition studies and uses them. That
is — independently — the exact reverent / fearful / pragmatic axis in
HUMANS_DESIGN.md. Convergent design is usually a sign the axis is right.

### The Kalos war and the ultimate weapon [CANON]

The darkest thing in the entire series, and the best precedent for "there
was a before, and it was catastrophic":

- **3000 years ago there was a war.** King **AZ** built an ultimate weapon
  to end it — powered by **the life force of Pokémon**, consuming enormous
  numbers of them.
- He built it partly to resurrect his own **Floette**, which had died in
  that war. It worked. The Floette, understanding what had been done to
  bring it back, **left him.**
- AZ was made **immortal** and wandered for three thousand years looking for
  it. You meet him as an enormous, ancient man.
- **Mega Evolution stones** are debris from the weapon's blast.

A war fought by consuming Pokémon lives, a grief-driven resurrection, and a
partner who walks away from what you did for it. In a children's game.

### Unova's founding myth [CANON]

- There was **one original dragon**. Twin brothers who ruled together
  quarrelled — one pursuing **truth**, one pursuing **ideals** — and the
  dragon split into **Reshiram** and **Zekrom** to take a side each.
- **Kyurem** is what was left over: the empty husk, missing whatever the
  other two took.

A creation myth where the gods are the wreckage of a family argument.

### Ancient humans who could bind legendaries [CANON]

Recurring across regions, and the strongest support for "someone knew how
to do this once":

- **The Regis were sealed away** by ancient people, behind puzzles written
  in **Braille** — a real in-universe ancient script. People who could
  *contain* legendary Pokémon.
- **The Ruins of Alph** (Johto) — Unown, ancient chambers, writing nobody
  can read any more.
- **Solaceon Ruins**, **Tanoby Ruins**, the **Sealed Chamber**, the
  **Sinjoh Ruins** (where Arceus can create Dialga/Palkia/Giratina eggs).
- **The Dragon Clan** of Blackthorn — a surviving lineage with inherited
  traditions about how dragon Pokémon must be raised. One of the few places
  where old knowledge is shown as *continuous* rather than lost.

### Sinnoh's creation myth and its folk stories [CANON]

- **Arceus** hatched from an egg in nothingness and created **Dialga**
  (time), **Palkia** (space) and **Giratina** (antimatter). Giratina was
  banished for violence into the Distortion World.
- The **lake trio** — Uxie, Mesprit, Azelf — gave humanity **knowledge**,
  **emotion** and **willpower**.
- **The Canalave library folk tales** are the most useful lines in the
  franchise for us: they say that humans and Pokémon **once ate at the same
  table**, and that **people and Pokémon used to marry each other**.

### Paradox Pokémon and Area Zero [CANON, Gen 9]

The most recent excavation of the deepest layer, and conceptually the
boldest: **Area Zero**, an enormous crater in Paldea, holds **paradox
Pokémon** — creatures from the distant past and the far future that are
recognisably *versions* of modern species while being genuinely alien.
Ancient ones are brutal and primal; future ones are metallic and machine-
like. A research expedition into the crater went badly wrong.

What makes this useful to us isn't the time-travel framing, it's the
implication: **the world's deep past was populated by things that were not
quite the animals we know now.** For a world with generated history and
ruins, "what lived here before" is a legitimate and evocative question.

### Extinct and lost forms [CANON, Legends: Arceus]

A quieter but very usable idea from the same game: **Hisuian forms** are
regional variants that existed then and *don't any more*. Some evolutions
(Wyrdeer, Kleavor, Ursaluna, Basculegion, Sneasler, Overqwil) only happen
under Hisui's conditions and are effectively lost afterward.

The best gag in it also has real teeth: **Hisuian Voltorb resembles an old
apricorn Poké Ball**, where the modern one mimics a modern Poké Ball. The
species *imitates human technology*, and changed when the technology did.
That's a genuinely lovely piece of world-building — a Pokémon whose form is
a record of what humans were doing at the time.

### Other regions' deep past [CANON, lower confidence on detail]

- **Alola** — guardian deities (the tapu), an ancient conflict between
  islands, and **Necrozma**, which "lost its light" and takes it from
  others. Ultra Space holds **Ultra Megalopolis**, a civilisation living in
  permanent darkness after losing its sun.
- **Galar** — the **Darkest Day**, ~3000 years ago, when **Eternatus**
  arrived and two heroes with Zacian and Zamazenta stopped it. Stone circles
  and barrows across the region.

**Explicit uncertainty**: for the very newest titles I'd rather flag a gap
than invent one. If there's more recent deep-past material (a Legends game
after Arceus, for instance), treat this file as incomplete on that point
rather than authoritative.

### Pokopia [CANON, spinoff] — the far end of the bracket

*Research supplied by the project owner (Sept 2026), not from my own
knowledge — the base game released March 2026, right at the edge of what I
can speak to, and the expansion material (announced June 2026, first part
August 2026) is past it entirely. Treat this section as their account, which
is more reliable than mine would have been.*

**Pokémon Pokopia** (Switch 2, Game Freak + Omega Force, March 2026) — a
cozy life-sim in **post-apocalyptic Kanto**. No battling, no levelling, no
evolution. And it is, thematically, the exact mirror of what we're building.

**The catastrophe.** A slow-building climate collapse — worsening storms,
rising seas — forced humanity to **evacuate off-world**, intending to return
once Earth stabilised. Contact was lost. Hundreds of years have passed. Two
details make it much darker than the cozy surface suggests:
- **Team Rocket built the evacuation rockets** — a genuinely bleak pun.
- **The trip was one-way.** Insufficient return fuel, and silence from the
  colony ever since.

**You are the last "human," and you aren't one.** The player is a **Ditto**
that took the shape of its missing trainer *from memory* — wearing a ghost.
The backstory comes out through diary pages, newspaper scraps and letters —
and **the Pokémon around you cannot read human writing**, so you are the
only one who ever learns what happened. A deliberately melancholy thing to
put under a cozy varnish.

**The PC as an ark.** The Pokémon Storage System is retconned into a literal
survival vault — the **Pokémon Conservation Project**, housed at Silph Co.
HQ — which held Pokémon in stasis with a failsafe to release them once a
biome registered as livable again. That's *why* habitat restoration gates
progress: every biome you heal literally releases a batch of stored Pokémon
back into the world. A tidy piece of retroactive logic for a mechanic that
never had an in-universe explanation.

**Biomes map 1:1 onto ruined Kanto cities** — Withered Wasteland (Fuchsia),
Bleak Beach (Vermilion), Rocky Ridges (Pewter), Sparkling Skylands
(Celadon/Saffron), Palette Town (Pallet). The places kept working; the names
didn't survive.

#### Pokémon society after the collapse

With combat removed, Game Freak had to invent a replacement social layer,
and what they built is a **civic labour economy**:

- Every Pokémon has a **Specialty** derived from its typing/moveset — Build
  (Cubone, Timburr, Heracross — the carpenters), Bulldoze, Engineering
  (Magnemite and kin), Gather/Chop/Recycle, Water, and singleton jobs
  (Smearguru paints, Chef Dente cooks, Porygon-Z refines, **Professor
  Tangrowth appraises**).
- **Role-based, not hierarchical.** No alpha, no dominance structure — an
  ant-colony/guild economy rather than a pack. Notably, **no aggression and
  no territory disputes at all**: cohabitation is the default once comfort
  needs are met, a real departure from mainline encounter design.
- **You don't command specialties.** A Pokémon just does its job when near
  the right task. The design claim is explicit: **instinct *is* the job**,
  not something taught.
- **Comfort, not capture.** Habitats are furniture arrangements (200+
  configurations), and Pokémon self-select into them based on habitat type,
  weather and time of day. You attract residents by building conditions they
  would choose, then offer favourite food to earn trust.
- **Professor Tangrowth** is the sole elder figure — the last non-Ditto
  resident found alive, alone in the wasteland for years, positioned as a
  surviving witness rather than an authority.
- **Legendaries sit outside the labour economy entirely** — visitors, not
  residents. Ho-Oh and Lugia pass overhead in the right weather and drop
  feathers; Dream Islands hold the roaming beasts; Mewtwo is a fixed
  encounter on the Celadon Department Store rooftop (a nod to the original
  dev-room placement). Forces that pass through and bless the world.

#### The individual backstories are the dark part

Named NPCs carry real biography tied to the apocalypse:
- **Peakychu** — a Pikachu who spent all her electricity trying to heal sick
  friends during the crisis, permanently draining herself. Her fur turned
  ghostly white.
- **Mosslax** — a Snorlax who tried to sleep off a bad mood and slept
  through the entire apocalypse, waking up overgrown with moss and vines.
- **Professor Tangrowth** — waited faithfully at his post for humans who
  were never coming back, holding out hope against the fuel logs and the
  silence.

#### Confirmed additional material

- **Expansion Pass** (announced June 2026): Part 1 **Bubbly Basin** (Aug
  2026) added an underwater town and Dive, taught to Ditto by Manaphy —
  another biome recovering in parallel. Part 2 (late 2026) is cosmetics;
  Part 3 (2027) another town. **Nothing so far expands the humanity-side
  mystery** — additive worldbuilding, not plot.
- The **Sparkling Skylands hidden room** turned out to be a developer-room
  easter egg (a homage to the Gen 1 Celadon dev room), **not** a lore
  payoff about humanity's fate.

#### Still open (from the source notes)

- The Cubone "grave with flowers" habitat beat, and the Paras/Parasect
  dialogue exchange — both flagged as lore-relevant, detail not yet pulled.
- Whether species-level social-structure flavour text (herd sizes, pack
  structure) exists as a documented pattern — genuinely unresolved rather
  than confirmed absent.
- Re-check after Expansion Parts 2 and 3.
- A circulating claim that Pokémon "haven't been part of the world since
  ancient times" (citing Magcargo flavour text) is **fan speculation**, not
  dev-stated canon. [THEORY] at best.

### The bracket, now complete

With Pokopia confirmed, the franchise reads as one long arc about proximity
and distance between two kinds of life:

| Era | Human–Pokémon relationship |
|---|---|
| Celestica / Sinnoh folk tales | Once close — "ate at the same table" |
| **Our game** | Apart. Feared, revered, kept at distance. Partnership about to be reinvented |
| Hisui | Partnership newly, awkwardly beginning |
| Mainline | Partnership normalised and industrialised |
| Pokopia | Humans gone. Pokémon inherit the world, and the only "human" is a Ditto wearing a dead man's shape |

**We are deliberately sited at the moment before the turn** — and it's worth
knowing that's a choice, not a default.

## Spinoffs that already did large parts of what we're doing

Two of these are closer to this project than any mainline game, and both
were easy to overlook because they're spinoffs.

### Pokémon Conquest [CANON-ish, spinoff] — bonding without capture, already shipped

A Nobunaga's Ambition crossover set in **Ransei**, a feudal-Japanese region
of 17 kingdoms, each aligned to a type. Two things make it directly
relevant:

- **There are no Poké Balls. The entire mechanic is bonding.** A warlord
  forms a **link** with a Pokémon, and that link has a real numeric strength
  that grows through fighting and acting together. Link strength drives
  stats and gates evolution. Warlords have **perfect links** with particular
  species — a specific person and a specific kind of Pokémon who suit each
  other.
- **Ransei is shaped like Arceus**, and was created by it; uniting all 17
  kingdoms summons the legendary.

So the "no capture, a relationship that deepens through shared action, with
a compatibility element" design is not only viable, it's shipped and it's
the best precedent we have for what our rapport graph is being asked to do.
The affinity idea — that certain people and certain species *fit* — is worth
considering for our bonding stages.

### Pokémon Mystery Dungeon [CANON-ish, spinoff] — this is the genre precedent

**PMD is a roguelike.** It's built on Chunsoft's Mystery Dungeon lineage
(the Shiren games), which means the closest thing to "a Pokémon roguelike"
already exists and has for two decades. Worth knowing in detail rather than
rediscovering:

Mechanics it already has that we're proposing:
- **Floor-by-floor dungeons with stairs** — our cave, more or less exactly.
- **A belly/hunger meter** that drains as you move and must be managed.
- **Recruitment** — a defeated Pokémon can choose to join your team.
- **Party command** — you direct AI teammates with tactics and orders,
  which is the same UX problem the pitch already flags as hard.
- Item management, throwables, weather inside dungeons, traps.

Its world is also a genuine inversion worth thinking about: **there are no
humans.** Pokémon have towns, guilds, shops and a society; humans are
mythical. The player is a human who has been *turned into* a Pokémon and
doesn't remember it.

Its story is the darkest in the franchise outside AZ. In Explorers of
Time/Darkness/Sky: **time itself stops**. The **Temporal Tower** collapses,
and the future becomes a frozen, lightless world where Pokémon have lost
their emotions — **Primal Dialga** raging at the centre of it. Characters
travel back to prevent it knowing they'll be erased if they succeed.

**Where we differ, and should stay differing**: PMD's dungeons are pure
dungeon (no ecology, no settlement, no world simulation between runs), its
world is authored, and it has no humans. Ours is an ecosystem simulation
with a generated world, a settled human layer, and a fragile human
protagonist. The overlap is the *dungeon grammar* — floors, stairs, hunger,
a commanded partner — which is worth borrowing knowingly rather than
converging on by accident.

### The two wars, side by side

Worth putting these next to each other, since they're the same idea forty
years apart:

- **The fan war theory [THEORY]** — Lt. Surge's "electric Pokémon saved me
  during the war," a Kanto conspicuously short of men of that age, derelict
  buildings. Never confirmed, but it's the fandom's oldest serious reading
  of the setting, and it's about a *recent* war.
- **The Kalos war [CANON, Gen 6]** — 3000 years ago, ended by AZ's ultimate
  weapon, which consumed Pokémon lives as fuel. Explicit, on-screen, with a
  named immortal survivor still walking around.

The interesting part: **the fandom's instinct was right in shape and wrong
in time.** They read a world that had clearly survived something terrible;
canon eventually confirmed a war of exactly that character, just pushed
three millennia back. For our purposes that's permission — a world whose
history includes something catastrophic that people only half-remember is
squarely canonical, and it's the natural home for our lost-knowledge and
relic threads.

## Leaks and unverified material — handle with care

Asked about directly ("the weird rumored leaks from the last few years that
had some strange creation myths and stuff"), so here's an honest answer
rather than a confident one.

**What's real that I'm confident about:**

- **The 1997 Spaceworld demo leak** — a genuine build of prototype Gold/
  Silver surfaced years ago, containing beta designs, unused Pokémon and
  earlier versions of familiar ones. Real, well documented, and interesting
  mostly as design archaeology.
- **The 2024 Game Freak breach** (widely called the "Teraleak") — a large
  internal data breach that circulated source code, development material and
  design/concept documents. That it happened, and that lore-adjacent
  material was among it, is widely reported.

**What I won't do is repeat specifics I can't stand behind.** There are
circulated claims about creation-myth material, early notes on where Pokémon
come from, and human–Pokémon origin concepts. I have only fuzzy awareness of
these, and leak summaries get distorted heavily as they spread —
paraphrase-of-a-paraphrase is how "an early concept document" becomes "the
secret true canon." I'd rather leave a gap here than fabricate a myth and
have it end up load-bearing in our design.

**Three practical reasons not to build on this material**, beyond accuracy:

1. **It isn't canon.** Cut concepts and internal drafts are, by definition,
   things that were not chosen. Their being unused is often the most
   informative fact about them.
2. **It's unstable ground.** If a detail turns out to be misattributed or
   invented, anything we built on it has to be unpicked.
3. **It's legally and reputationally messy** for a project that might ever
   be public — which matters more than it would for a private thought
   experiment.

**And the honest kicker: we don't need it.** Everything this project wants
from the lore — a lost civilisation, a catastrophe behind present-day power,
an era when people and Pokémon were closer, a frontier where partnership is
new — is *already in published canon*, sitting in the Canalave library, the
Ruins of Alph, AZ's story and Hisui. The published material is richer than
the rumours, and we can cite it.

If you want to chase specific leak claims, my suggestion is to treat them as
**inspiration you re-derive independently** — if an idea is good, write our
own version from our own world's generated history, and don't source it.

## Gen 1 / 2: the dark stuff hiding in a kids' game [CANON]

- **The Mewtwo journals, Pokémon Mansion.** You explore a burnt-out
  abandoned lab on Cinnabar and read diary entries: a new Pokémon found deep
  in the jungle in Guyana, South America, christened **Mew**; then "Mew gave
  birth. We named the newborn **Mewtwo**"; then it grew too powerful, they
  failed to curb its tendencies, and it destroyed the place you're standing
  in. Nobody comments on it. You just read it and leave.
- **Lavender Town.** An entire town that exists to bury Pokémon. In the
  Tower is a **Marowak ghost** you cannot even fight until you find the
  Silph Scope — killed by Team Rocket while protecting her Cubone. And
  **Cubone's own dex entry** says it wears the skull of its dead mother and
  cries at the moon. The two entries are the same story told from both ends.
- **Team Rocket's actual crimes are mundane**, which makes them worse:
  cutting tails off **Slowpoke** to sell as food, and that one killing. Not
  world domination — poaching and extortion.
- **Pollution Pokémon.** Grimer, Muk, Koffing and Weezing are born from
  industrial sludge and gas. **Porygon** is explicitly man-made — "the first
  Pokémon created by humans," made of programmable code.
- **The Burned Tower.** Three Pokémon died in the fire at the Brass Tower in
  Ecruteak roughly 150 years before Gen 2. **Ho-Oh resurrected them** as
  Raikou, Entei and Suicune — the disaster-and-renewal arc DESIGN.md's
  geological vision already leans on. That's real canon, not our invention.
- **People eat Pokémon.** Slowpoke tails are sold as a delicacy; Farfetch'd
  is hunted. The games are quiet about it but never deny it.

## Fan theories, tiered by how well they actually hold up

**Genuinely well-supported [THEORY]:**

- **Your rival's Raticate dies.** He battles you with one aboard the S.S.
  Anne. You win. The next time you meet him it is *gone from his team* — and
  that meeting is inside **Pokémon Tower**, the graveyard. Nothing is ever
  stated. Everything points at it.
- **The war.** Lt. Surge says electric Pokémon saved him "during the war."
  Kanto is conspicuously short of men of that age. The reading: a war killed
  a generation, which is why children are sent out alone and why so much of
  the region is derelict. One line of dialogue doing enormous work.

**Fun but circumstantial [THEORY]:**

- **Ditto is a failed Mew clone** — both Transform, and Ditto turns up
  around Cinnabar Island, right where the Mewtwo lab was.
- **Cubone is a baby Kangaskhan** — largely debunked, but it was *the*
  theory for years.
- **Gengar is Clefable's shadow** — matching silhouettes, and Gengar is
  literally the Shadow Pokémon.

**Not real, but culturally real [FICTION]:**

- **Lavender Town Syndrome** — the story that the original track's
  frequencies drove children to suicide. Fabricated, and the most famous
  piece of fan mythology the series has.

## What we should actually steal

### 1. The dex is an unreliable narrator — make that a feature

Entries flatly contradict each other between versions; the same species is a
harmless forager in one and a soul-eater in another. For a world with
**generated history and per-settlement culture**, that's not sloppiness to
avoid — it's the target. Two villages holding contradictory, equally sincere
beliefs about the same species is *canon-accurate*, and it falls naturally
out of the reverent/fearful/pragmatic attitude axis plus generated history.

### 2. "There was a before, and it was lost" is the franchise's oldest note

Celestica ruins under Hisui, the Regi seals, Braille, the Ruins of Alph,
AZ's weapon. It recurs in every region. This is the canon backing for:

- **TMs as ancient found relics** rather than crafted goods.
- **"You're the first trainer"** meaning *first in living memory* — someone
  knew, and it went. Much stronger than first-ever.
- **Ruins as a real generated feature** with real recorded causes, which our
  history pass produces anyway.

### 3. Nobles and wardens — the best single mechanic to borrow

A revered local Pokémon, tended by a human who has never *owned* it, that
can go **frenzied** and must be **calmed rather than killed**. That is:

- A perfect fit for our **reverent** settlements.
- A natural "befriending a predator is hard but possible" set piece — the
  pitch already wants exactly this for the later cave layers.
- A quest shape that isn't combat, for a player whose partner needs
  non-combat jobs.
- Structurally close to what `herdConflict.ts` already produces (a pressured
  animal behaving badly for real reasons).

### 4. "Humans and Pokémon once ate at the same table"

The single most useful canon line for our premise. It means a pre-trainer
world of distance and reverence isn't a departure from the lore — it's a
plausible **middle** of a story the canon already tells: they were close,
then they weren't, then (with the player) they begin to be again.

### 5. Conquest's "link" is the closest thing to our bonding design

A numeric bond that grows through *acting together*, gates progression
(evolution), and has a compatibility element — certain people suit certain
species. Our rapport graph is already the data structure for this; what
Conquest adds is the idea that **affinity is a real, unevenly-distributed
thing**, so who you can bond with easily is itself a fact about your
character. Worth considering as a differentiator from a flat "anyone can
bond with anything if they grind it."

### 6. Mystery Dungeon already solved several of our UX problems

Since PMD is the direct genre precedent (a Pokémon roguelike with floors,
stairs, a hunger meter, recruitment and a commanded AI partner), its
solutions are worth studying before we invent our own — particularly its
**party command UX**, which is exactly the problem the pitch flags as hard
("gotta make ux easy here"). Borrow knowingly; differ where our simulation
gives us something PMD never had (a real ecology, a settled world, and
consequences that persist between visits).

### 7. Pokopia's "instinct is the job" — use it, but split by species

Pokopia derives a Pokémon's civic role from its typing and moveset:
personality *is* the job, nothing is taught. HUMANS_DESIGN.md independently
proposed the opposite for humans — roles **earned** through accumulated
history, so the person who spent their life building *becomes* the builder.

Both are right, for different species, and the contrast is worth making
load-bearing:

> **Pokémon are what they are. Humans become what they do.**

That's a clean, cheap rule that says something real about the world, and it
gives us two role systems that don't compete: species-derived specialties on
one side (which our `SpeciesDef` typing/`preferredTerrain` data could
already drive), earned notable titles on the other (which `notables.ts`
already does).

### 8. "Comfort, not capture" — and the endgame it implies

Pokopia's attraction loop is: build the conditions a Pokémon would choose,
then offer food to earn trust. No capture anywhere in it. That's strong
validation for our bonding design, but the more interesting part is what it
suggests for our *late* game.

Our villages currently keep Pokémon **out** — walls, watchfires, distance.
The player is the first person who can do the opposite. So: **the player
eventually builds a place where both can live**, and Pokémon start choosing
to be there. That's a mechanically real expression of "you're the first,"
it's the natural Act 2/3 payoff of the whole premise, and the machinery is
mostly present already (`speciesFitsZone`/`estimateZoneSpecies` decide where
a species belongs; the player would be *changing the inputs*).

The in-fiction line from Pokopia players is almost exactly our thesis: if
building good habitats brings Pokémon back, maybe building a good town
brings people back too.

### 9. Knowledge that is physically present but socially lost

The best melancholy detail in Pokopia: the Pokémon can't read human writing,
so the player is the only one who ever learns the truth. Invert it for us:
**ruins hold records nobody in the village can read any more** — which is
already canon-shaped (Braille in the Regi chambers, Unown at the Ruins of
Alph) and pairs perfectly with our generated history. The information
survives; the ability to interpret it didn't.

Also worth stealing: **places outlive their names.** Pokopia's biomes are
ruined Kanto cities under new names. Our `territories.ts` naming plus a
history pass could hold *two* names for a place — the current one, and an
older one that only appears in records and ruins.

### 10. Recontextualising a mechanic as lore is the house move

Pokopia retcons the storage PC into a literal survival vault with a
livability failsafe — giving a decades-old convenience mechanic a real
in-world reason. That is precisely the move behind **TMs-as-ancient-relics**,
and it suggests the technique generalises: any mechanic we ship can be given
an in-world cause by our history pass rather than existing as pure UI.

### 11. Irreplaceable individuals vs. inherited offices

Pokopia has **singleton specialties** — only Tangrowth appraises, only
Smearguru paints — which quietly makes those individuals personally known
and irreplaceable. We decided the opposite for humans (inherited roles, so
*the elder* is an office that survives the person).

Both are good; they just serve different feelings. Suggested split:
**inherited offices for humans** (resilient, keeps quests alive, and someone
having to step up is itself a story) and **irreplaceability for notable
Pokémon** (a specific revered individual whose loss is permanent and
grievable). That also lines up neatly with the nobles/wardens idea above.

### 12. What we deliberately do NOT take from Pokopia

Pokopia removes aggression and territory disputes entirely — cohabitation is
the default. That's the right call for a cozy game and exactly the wrong one
for us: **predation, territory and resource conflict are the engine of our
whole simulation.** Our world is one where a herd in the barley is a real
problem, and that friction is what makes the eventual coexistence mean
anything. Worth naming so we don't drift cozy by accident.

### 13. Environmental, unexplained storytelling

The best old lore is archaeological, not expository. You learn the darkest
thing in Gen 1 by reading a diary in an abandoned building, and no character
ever mentions it. Our generated history plus ruins plus chronicle is
literally a machine for producing that — the discipline to steal is
**restraint**: put the record in the world and let the player find it, don't
have an NPC explain it.

---

## Where we should deliberately differ from Hisui

Given how close the premises are, the differences should be chosen, not
accidental:

| Legends: Arceus | Ours |
|---|---|
| Poké Balls exist, newly invented | **No capture at all** — bonding is relational, through the rapport graph |
| Authored, fixed world | **Generated world with generated history** |
| Real-time action catching | **Turn-based roguelike** |
| No settlement simulation | **Farming, trade, motivations, settlements that grow and fall** |
| You're an outsider sent by a god | **You're nobody** — desperate and alone in a cave, first because you had no walls to hide behind |

The last row is the one I'd protect hardest. "Chosen by Arceus" and
"survived by reading an animal correctly" are very different games, and ours
is more interesting.
